When people talk about design systems, the conversation almost always starts with components. Teams discuss buttons, forms, layout primitives, tokens, and variants. Roadmaps get built around shipping components and expanding the library. Many organizations even measure the maturity of a design system by the number of components it provides.

But after working with design systems for some time, you begin to notice something important.

Components are rarely the real problem.

Most teams can eventually build good components. Given enough time, designers and engineers will define tokens, create component patterns, implement them in code, and document them. The technical part can be difficult, but it is usually solvable.

The real challenge begins once the system exists and multiple teams start using it.

Because the hardest part of a design system was never building components. The hardest part has always been **people, coordination and adoption across teams**.

## Adoption is the real design system problem

Many design systems look successful when you evaluate them purely from a library perspective. They contain dozens of components, clear documentation and well structured tokens. From the outside everything looks organized.

But inside organizations the reality is often very different.

Product teams are building features under tight timelines. Designers are solving specific product problems. Engineers are optimizing for delivery speed. In that environment, teams do not always think about the design system first. They think about solving their problem quickly.

This is where adoption becomes the real challenge.

A team might slightly modify a component to solve a local problem. Another team might rebuild the same component in code because it feels faster. A third team might request a completely new pattern because the existing system does not fit their use case.

None of these actions are malicious. They usually happen because the system does not yet fit the team’s needs perfectly.

Over time, however these small deviations begin to accumulate. Different teams start building slightly different solutions to similar problems. The system slowly loses the consistency it was supposed to provide.

At this stage, the problem is not about missing components anymore. The problem becomes **how the organization collectively uses the system**.

## Education becomes more important than components

Once a design system is used across multiple teams, the role of the system team starts to shift. Instead of spending all their time creating new UI elements, they begin spending more time helping people understand how to use the system correctly.

This educational work rarely appears on a design system roadmap but it consumes a large portion of the team’s time.

Design system teams answer questions like:

- Which component should we use in this situation?
- Can this component be modified for a specific product need?
- Should this pattern live in the core system or only in our product?
- How do we implement this component correctly in code?

These questions come from designers, engineers and product managers across the organization. Each team operates in a slightly different context, which means the system needs explanation and guidance.

Many design system teams eventually realize that they are not only building a system. They are also **running an internal education program**.

Workshops, documentation updates, office hours, design reviews and direct conversations become part of the job. The goal is not just to provide components but to help teams understand how the system should evolve alongside their product work.

## Multiple teams create competing priorities

Another challenge that appears quickly in large organizations is the number of requests that come from different teams at the same time.

A design system team might receive requests such as:

- A product team asking for a new component needed for a feature launch
- Another team requesting improvements to an existing pattern
- Engineers requesting performance improvements in the code library
- Designers asking for more flexibility in certain components

Every request may be valid. Each team is solving a real product problem.

But the system team cannot build everything at once. This creates one of the most difficult aspects of system work: **prioritization across the organization**.

The system team must evaluate which requests benefit the broader ecosystem and which requests are too specific to a single product. They must decide whether a new component belongs in the core system or should remain a local solution.

These decisions require a deep understanding of the product landscape across the organization.

Sometimes the correct answer is to build a new component. Sometimes the correct answer is to encourage a team to work within existing patterns. And sometimes the correct answer is simply that the system team cannot prioritize that request right now.

## Transparency becomes critical

One of the biggest frustrations teams experience when working with a design system is not knowing why certain decisions were made.

A product team may request a component and feel frustrated when it does not appear on the roadmap. Without context, it can look like the system team is blocking progress.

This is why transparency becomes extremely important in system work.

Design system teams need to communicate clearly about:

- Why certain components are prioritized
- Why some requests cannot be addressed immediately
- How decisions are made about adding patterns to the system

Transparency helps teams understand that the system is not ignoring their needs. It is simply balancing the needs of many teams across the organization.

This level of openness also helps build trust. Teams are more likely to work with the system if they understand how decisions are made.

## Flexibility is harder than it sounds

Another tension that design system teams constantly manage is the balance between consistency and flexibility.

If a system is too rigid, product teams may feel blocked. They may struggle to solve new problems because the system does not allow enough variation.

On the other hand, if a system is too flexible, it loses its purpose. Teams begin creating too many variations, which leads to inconsistency across the product.

Finding the right balance between structure and flexibility requires ongoing conversations with product teams. It also requires a willingness to evolve the system as new patterns emerge.

Often the best systems are not the ones with the most components. They are the ones that provide a strong foundation while still allowing teams to innovate when necessary.

## Design systems operate inside organizations, not design tools

One misconception about design systems is that they are purely technical or design artifacts. In reality, they are deeply tied to how organizations collaborate.

Large companies often end up with multiple systems or subsystems that evolve over time. Different product areas may build their own extensions of the core system. These systems must eventually coordinate with each other to avoid duplication and confusion.

Managing this coordination requires agreements between teams about how systems interact, how components are shared, and how updates are communicated. Without these agreements, teams can accidentally step on each other’s work or create competing versions of the same patterns.

This kind of coordination work is not about UI components. It is about **relationships between teams**.

## The system team becomes a connector

Because of these challenges, the role of a design system team often evolves beyond design and engineering.

System teams become connectors inside the organization. They talk to product teams, engineering groups, and leadership. They help different teams align around shared patterns and standards.

In many ways, the system team acts as a bridge between multiple parts of the organization.

They translate product needs into reusable patterns. They help engineers implement those patterns correctly. They coordinate changes across systems and ensure that updates do not break existing products.

None of this work is visible when someone looks at a component library. But it is the work that keeps the system healthy.

## Components are only the visible layer

When people first encounter a design system, they usually see the visible artifacts. They see the components, tokens, and documentation. It is easy to assume that building these artifacts is the most difficult part of the system.

In reality, those artifacts are only the visible layer.

Behind them is a continuous effort to align teams, prioritize requests, educate users, and evolve patterns as products grow. This work requires communication, patience, and a deep understanding of how the organization builds software.

That is why the hardest part of design systems was never components.

The hardest part has always been **helping an entire organization build products in a shared and coordinated way**.
