## From consumer to creator to system architect, tokens, components, and org-wide impact

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*BDRqHYUk4fQ6ROOFne15pw.jpeg)

Design systems are the infrastructure of design at scale. They are what allow a product used by millions to feel coherent, a team of fifty designers to move quickly, and a new engineer to ship UI on day one without asking ten questions. The designers and managers who build and maintain these systems occupy one of the highest-leverage roles in any mature product organisation.

Yet the design system career path is also one of the least formalized. Many organisations treat design systems work as a rotation, a side project, or a team-of-one. This guide builds a rigorous career ladder for the discipline — from an Associate who consumes the system all the way to a Principal who defines it at business-unit level, and a Design System Manager who leads the team responsible for the foundation that everyone else builds on.

> *“A design system is never finished. It is always either growing toward the product’s needs or falling behind them. The designers who understand this build different systems than the ones who think they’re building a final product.”*

## What design system work is and isn’t

Design system work sits at the intersection of product design, visual design, front-end engineering, and documentation. It is not just building component libraries in Figma. It is making decisions about naming, hierarchy, constraints, and flexibility that will shape how every designer and engineer in the organisation works. At senior levels, it is fundamentally a governance and advocacy role: deciding what goes into the system, what stays out, and how to maintain it as the product evolves under operational pressure.

## IC1: Associate Design System Designer

What you own: Using the existing design system correctly and consistently. Applying tokens, components, and patterns in your assigned design work. Flagging inconsistencies or gaps when you encounter them. Learning the rationale behind system decisions — not just what they are, but why.

How you work: Closely supervised. You are embedded with a product team and are the system’s advocate in that team, while learning the system’s architecture from the design system team.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*PchFX3uFVLGDFcqU0gzTyw.png)

**ℹ️ Growth focus:** Build a habit of asking “why” about every system decision. Understanding the rationale behind a component’s constraints will make you a better contributor than any amount of Figma proficiency alone. Start developing your documentation instinct — clear, useful notes about decisions are a core design system skill.

## IC2: Design System Designer

What you own: Adding new components and expanding existing patterns based on product needs. Working with product designers to translate product-specific UI solutions into reusable system components. Improving and optimising existing components based on usage patterns and feedback. Writing and maintaining documentation for your contributions. Collaborating with front-end engineers on design-to-code translation.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*BqjIv_kmlVyT5Ydzp9YJWA.png)

**ℹ️ Growth focus:** The key skill at IC2 is knowing what belongs in the system and what doesn’t. Not every UI solution should be abstracted into a component. Building the judgment to distinguish a genuinely reusable pattern from a one-off product decision is what separates a good design system designer from one who accidentally bloats the system.

## IC3: Senior Design System Designer I

What you own: Architecting whole domains of the design system (e.g., the entire form system, the navigation system, the data visualisation layer). Defining contribution guidelines and reviewing external contributions from product teams. Leading the token architecture — naming conventions, semantic layers, multi-brand adaptability. Creating the motion design system. Advocating for system adoption across product teams and educating designers on correct usage.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*DSpRpdgULVkZH0mrhEEmXw.png)

**ℹ️ Growth focus:** At IC3, the system’s architecture is your responsibility. Start developing a strong point of view on token naming and hierarchy — semantic token systems (referencing purpose rather than value) are significantly more maintainable at scale. Build your front-end fluency: knowing how your Figma decisions translate into CSS variables and React props makes your system dramatically more implementable.

## IC4: Senior Design System Designer II (Staff)

What you own: Owning the system architecture across multiple products or brands. Setting the standards for how components are built, documented, and versioned across the organisation. Leading system migration projects. Defining the design system roadmap in alignment with product roadmaps. Actively managing relationships with engineering leads, product directors, and brand teams. Running system office hours and contribution review processes. Educating the design organisation on design system thinking.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*uuCrmj1cJpF6s89or5FsCw.png)

**ℹ️ Growth focus:** IC4 is where design system work becomes governance work. The hardest problem at this level isn’t building good components — it’s deciding how the system evolves: what gets deprecated, what gets added, how you handle the tension between consistency and product team autonomy. Start building your advocacy strategy: a system no one uses is worse than no system at all.

## IC5: Principal Design System Designer

What you own: Defining the design system strategy for the entire design organisation, including multi-brand and multi-platform considerations. Setting the technical and philosophical standards for how design tokens, components, and patterns are structured at the business-unit level. Mentoring senior designers and engineers on design system thinking. Representing the discipline externally — speaking, writing, and community involvement. Anticipating the future needs of the system based on product direction and technology evolution.

## Management track: Design System Manager

## M1: Design System Manager

What you own: Leading the design system team — typically a mix of design system designers and front-end engineers. Building the team through hiring and onboarding. Setting the quality bar for contributions, reviews, and documentation. Defining the system roadmap in alignment with product priorities, engineering architecture, and brand direction. Running the governance process: deciding what enters the system, what gets deprecated, and how contribution works. Being the primary stakeholder contact for the system across design, engineering, and product leadership. Measuring the system’s impact — adoption, coverage, contribution velocity, time-to-ship for consumer teams.

Time allocation: ~30% team leadership · 40% system strategy and governance · 30% stakeholder management and evangelism.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*Gnm9PV-5Me7vammXQvMprQ.png)

**ℹ️ Growth focus:** A Design System Manager’s biggest challenge is the three-sided stakeholder problem: design teams want flexibility and novelty, engineering teams want stability and performance, and product leadership wants speed. Your job is to build a system and a process that serves all three simultaneously — and to be the person who explains clearly why all three can’t always win at once.

## M2: Senior Design System Manager

What you own: Multi-product or multi-brand design system strategy. Leading a team of managers and senior ICs. Defining the long-term technical and design direction of the system. Setting adoption KPIs across the business unit. Aligning system roadmap with CTO, CPO, and brand leadership. Driving the transition to token-based or multi-platform architecture when the business requires it.

## The hardest problems in design systems work

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*7Gwgr4N3uRpccq-xk8VzjQ.png)

Figure 1. The three core tensions in design systems work — all resolved through clear governance, not through better components.

Senior design system work is not primarily a craft problem. It is a coordination problem. The tension between consistency and flexibility, adoption and autonomy, velocity and quality cannot be solved by building better components. It can only be managed through clear governance: documented principles for what enters the system, explicit contribution processes, meaningful versioning, and stakeholder relationships built on trust and transparency.
