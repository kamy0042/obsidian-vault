Design Systems have stopped being component libraries. They are becoming the most important knowledge platform an organization owns — and AI is about to make that undeniably clear.

## The shift has already begun

For a long time, the success of a Design System was measured with relatively simple questions:

**How many components do we have?  
How many teams use the library?  
How much time do we save by reusing components?**

Those questions are still valid, but they no longer represent the real value a Design System can deliver.

The study [The Business Value of Design Systems](https://www.figma.com/reports/the-business-value-of-design-systems/), produced by the Design Executive Council (DXC) and sponsored by Figma, reveals a meaningful shift in how leading organizations understand Design Systems.

They are no longer seen merely as tools for maintaining visual consistency.

The most mature companies are treating them as infrastructure for scaling digital products, reducing operational costs, accelerating innovation, and supporting business growth. In that sense, Design Systems are starting to function as a backbone, as relevant to the digital experience as branding and communication are to brand identity.

This change in perspective is probably one of the most important our industry has seen in the last decade.

## The real problem was never the components

When an organization starts to grow, symptoms appear that we have all seen.

One team builds its own button; another creates a slightly different variant; a third product tweaks the colors; a fourth changes the spacing. Over time, dozens of versions of the same component appear.

The same happens with forms, tables, modals, navigation, error messages, validations, and practically every interaction pattern.

The result is familiar: more work, more technical debt, more development time, more errors, more inconsistencies, and greater difficulty maintaining the product.

But over the years, I came to understand that components were never the real problem.

The problem was the lack of a shared language. When design, engineering, product, and business make decisions using different rules, the cost grows exponentially.

A well-built Design System removes exactly that friction.

## The real value lies in the knowledge

When we think of a Design System, we usually picture a library of buttons, typography, iconography, colors, and components. But that is only the visible part.

Every component represents years of accumulated knowledge. Why does it exist? What problem does it solve? When should it be used, and when not? What accessibility criteria does it meet? What technical constraints does it have? What business decisions motivated its creation? What research supports that pattern?

All of that knowledge is usually scattered across documentation, meetings, Jira tickets, Figma files, and, above all, in people’s experience.

When those people leave the organization, much of that knowledge disappears with them. But good documentation alone is not enough either: even when it is visible and available, teams still face communication problems and often do not even know it exists.

Perhaps the most important asset of a Design System was never its components. It was always the knowledge those components represent.

## The numbers show this evolution is already happening

The DXC study reveals how the value of a Design System changes as it matures — and the data is compelling.

Among the leaders interviewed:

- **96%** believe a Design System improves brand consistency
- **92%** say it increases operational efficiency
- **92%** link it directly to faster time-to-market

But once the system reaches a higher level of maturity, far more strategic benefits begin to emerge: **scalability across multiple products, risk mitigation, business growth, and the capacity to innovate faster**.

In other words, the return on investment stops being measured only in hours saved. It starts being measured in business outcomes.

Real cases confirm this. Grammarly used its Design System to unify three different design languages after several acquisitions — reducing costs, accelerating integration, and freeing up roughly a quarter of the design and development teams’ work week. SAP has been evolving its system for more than a decade, today using it to maintain coherence across more than one hundred enterprise solutions, with its Chief Design Officer elevating it to a board-level indicator tracked through OKRs (Objectives and Key Results). Freshworks tied the evolution of its Design System directly to customer satisfaction indicators and cut support-related costs and resolution times by nearly a third.

The value is no longer limited to the design team. Its impact reaches operations, customer experience, and business results.

## Then Artificial Intelligence arrived

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*obqYreFtILF1-Y5XAWnGJw.jpeg)

The arrival of artificial intelligence changed the conversation.

Over the last two years, almost every discussion has centered on questions like:

**Will AI replace designers?  
Will it generate interfaces automatically?  
Will it be able to write code**?

These are interesting questions, but none of them points to the real change we are living through.

The truly important question is a different one: ***What happens when an artificial intelligence understands the knowledge contained in a Design System?***

Because at that moment it stops generating generic solutions. It starts working with an organization’s specific rules.

It no longer recommends just any component — it suggests the right one. It does not use just any color — it respects the design tokens the company has defined. It does not propose invented patterns — it uses the ones already validated with real users.

And this is not a future promise: it is already happening. Grammarly built a Figma linter that automatically checks whether a design meets the system’s standards. Notion exposes its Design System to AI agents through MCP servers, giving them controlled access to components, references, and production code. Freshworks uses AI to translate and summarize support tickets, extending the system’s consistency into the multilingual experience.

The Design System stops being a library. It becomes a source of knowledge for AI.

## The next evolution

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*GCbFFbj6KCKNy1AaGTLTiw.jpeg)

Until now, Design Systems have been excellent at storing components. But they are still relatively bad at sharing knowledge.

Finding information usually requires digging through documentation, searching multiple files, or asking specific people.

AI completely changes that scenario. Imagine some questions that could be answered instantly today:

- **“What is the recommended component for this flow?”**
- **“Is there a similar pattern that has already been used?”**
- **“Does this design meet the accessibility criteria?”**
- **“What changed between this version of the component and the previous one?”**
- **“Which token should I use in this case?”**
- **“Why was this component designed that way?”**

The answers stop depending on the designer who built the component three years ago. They become part of the shared knowledge of the entire organization.

And that difference completely changes the role of the Design System.

## A change that goes beyond design

One of the most interesting aspects of the DXC report is its argument that Artificial Intelligence will not only automate repetitive tasks.

It will also allow Design Systems to become platforms capable of sustaining quality, governance, accessibility, localization, and consistency at organizational scale.

That means their impact will no longer be limited to the daily work of designers and developers. It will begin to influence how companies make decisions, maintain standards, and build new products.

In other words, the Design System stops being a tool. It starts becoming part of the organization’s technology infrastructure.

## From why to how: where to start

Everything above answers one question: why invest strategically in a Design System. But a vision without a plan is just a manifesto. The question I hear most often from product and business leaders is a different one: how do we actually do it?

The cases in the study and my own experience building multi-brand systems suggest a path with six concrete moves.

**1\. Start small and grow in phases.** None of the companies in the study built their system all at once. SAP structured it in four layers — design language, components, patterns, and layouts — adopted progressively. Linear recommends building only what is needed at each moment, piece by piece, matching the effort to the organization’s maturity. The most common mistake is trying to launch the complete system before proving value.

2\. **Translate value into the language of the business.** Executives do not buy visual consistency; they buy speed, cost reduction, and risk mitigation. In a previous role, Grammarly’s Director of Product Design showed that a rollout that normally took three months could be completed in four days, with multi-million-dollar annual savings. That kind of narrative — time, money, risk — is what opens budgets.

3\. **Build a network of allies before asking for mandates.** Grammarly did not impose adoption: it earned it. It embedded designers in product teams, hired engineers dedicated to integration, ran listening tours with technical teams, and coupled the system with rebranding initiatives. A network of roughly ten champions across design and engineering acted as an extension of the core team. The clearest signal of success: it was engineering leaders who ended up asking for more investment in the system.

4\. **Define the system in code, not just in Figma.** Notion attributes its system’s resilience to a foundation defined directly in code, with tokens synced between design and development. A system that lives only in design files is a promise; one defined in code is infrastructure.

5\. **Measure in phases, not everything at once.** Freshworks designed a three-stage measurement strategy: first, cohesion of the product identity; then customer satisfaction (CSAT, task analysis, A/B testing); and finally developer productivity and satisfaction. Each stage matures alongside the system. Trying to measure everything from day one only produces empty metrics.

6\. **Document with AI in mind.** This is the move that connects practice with the vision of this article. Notion found that its investment in component rules and detailed documentation is exactly what makes its AI agents produce consistent, predictable results. Every decision documented today — the why behind a pattern, its constraints, its context — is knowledge that tomorrow can be queried, applied, and amplified by intelligent systems. Documentation is no longer hygiene: it is preparing the substrate AI will learn from.

None of these moves requires an extraordinary budget to begin. Above all, they require treating the system like a product: with a roadmap, with metrics, and with internal customers to serve.

## The metrics that will matter next — and why they matter now

This may be the part of the Design System conversation that deserves the most attention — and the least is being paid to it.

For a long time we talked about:

- **Number of components**
- **Adoption percentage**
- **Development speed**

But the next generation of Design Systems will likely be measured in fundamentally different terms — and that shift is probably less than two years away:

- **How much knowledge can the system answer on demand?**
- **How much does it shorten the onboarding of new collaborators?**
- **How many errors does it prevent before reaching production?**
- **How many decisions can be automated using AI?**
- **How many hours stop being spent searching for information?**

These are not vanity metrics. They represent a real transition: **from measuring what the system contains, to measuring what the system enables.**

Organizations that start defining these indicators today — before the tooling fully matures — will be the ones who can prove the value of their system when it matters most.

## Final reflection

For years we measured the value of a Design System by its components, its adoption, and the efficiency it generated for teams.

Today I understand that was only the visible part of a much deeper system.

The real asset was always the knowledge accumulated within them: the decisions, the rules, the experience, and the lessons learned.

Artificial intelligence will not replace that knowledge. It will make it accessible, connect it, and amplify it.

But there is something worth naming more precisely. AI is interesting not just because it stores or retrieves knowledge — it is interesting because it functions as a shared middle language between designer talk and engineer talk. It does not fully understand either discipline. But it understands enough of both to **bridge** the conversation between them.

In practice, that means AI does not just answer questions. It accelerates the conversations that used to get lost in translation — between what a designer intended and what an engineer implemented, between what a pattern means and what it should do in code.

A Design System trained on the right knowledge becomes the context that makes those conversations sharper, faster, and less dependent on the one person who was in the room when the decision was made.

The opportunity for organizations lies not only in documenting that knowledge better, but in turning it into a living capability: available to teams, connected to their working tools, and able to guide better decisions at scale.

That leap does not begin with a big investment. It begins with the next decision your team chooses to document.
