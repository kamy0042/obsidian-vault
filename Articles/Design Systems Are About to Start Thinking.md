Why the next version of your design system won’t just store things, it’ll help you decide things

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*uoRRSRFHoGLJ6UkVA5n-ig@2x.jpeg)

Photo by Balázs Kétyi on Unsplash

Design systems have been around for years now, and they’ve done a lot of good. They keep things consistent. They help teams move faster. They make sure the brand looks like the brand everywhere it shows up. They’re also the thing that finally got designers and engineers speaking the same language.

But if you’re honest about it, most design systems still just sit there. You go look something up in it. You copy a component. You check if you’re using the right spacing. It doesn’t do anything on its own, it just waits for you to come ask.

That’s starting to change.

The design system that just stores stuff

Right now, a typical design system is basically a really well-organized filing cabinet. It’s got your buttons, your colors, your spacing rules, your accessibility guidelines, your documentation. All good stuff. All useful.

But every time you start a new screen, you’re the one doing the thinking:

- Which component do I actually need here?
- \- What’s the right spacing again?
- \- Is this accessible?
- \- Did we already solve this exact problem somewhere else?

Here’s the annoying part: the answers are probably already sitting inside the design system somewhere. It just can’t tell you. It can’t talk back. You have to go dig for it yourself.

What if it could actually help?

Picture this instead. You’re starting a new project and instead of opening a blank file and hunting through documentation, you just describe what you’re trying to do:

“I need a payment confirmation screen that makes people feel like the payment went through safely, and nudges them to check out investing next.”

And instead of just showing you a list of components, the system actually understands what you’re asking for. It knows your design style, what your product is trying to do, your accessibility rules, what your engineers can realistically build, and how you’ve solved similar problems before. Then it gives you an actual suggestion, not just parts and pieces.

That’s the basic idea behind what people are starting to call an “agentic” design system. It doesn’t just hold information, it does something with it.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*kyGgRgTSJVnWgzr7Kd6QkQ@2x.jpeg)

Photo by Hal Gatewood on Unsplash

So what does that actually look like day to day?

A system like this would be able to:

Understand what you’re building. It knows the difference between an onboarding flow and a payments screen, and it adjusts its suggestions based on that.

Actually recommend something, not just list options. Instead of showing you every button in the library, it points you toward the right one for this specific situation, and tells you why.

Handle the boring, repetitive stuff. Building out different screen sizes, applying the right colors and spacing, making empty states and error messages, checking accessibility, writing up specs for engineers. All the stuff that eats up hours but doesn’t really need a human’s creative judgment.

Get smarter over time. Every time a design gets tested with real users, every time a review changes something, every bit of engineering feedback, it all gets fed back in. The system keeps learning instead of staying frozen the day someone built it.

A button stops being just a button

Right now, a component in a design system answers one question: what should this look like?

A smarter system answers a different question: what are we actually trying to get the user to do here?

So a button isn’t just a shape and a color anymore. It also carries some sense of how important it is, how risky the action is, whether it needs extra confirmation, whether it’s accessible for this context. The pieces stop being just visual building blocks and start carrying some actual understanding of the product around with them.

All that scattered knowledge finally has a home

Every team has a ton of design knowledge just floating around, and it’s usually a mess. Some of it’s in old Figma files. Some of it’s buried in a Slack thread from eight months ago. Some of it only exists in one engineer’s head because they remember a conversation from a design review.

A system like this pulls all of that into one place. Every decision your team makes becomes something the system can actually use again later, instead of getting forgotten the moment everyone moves on to the next project.

This doesn’t replace designers, it changes what they spend time on

It’s easy to hear all this and think it means designers become less necessary. History says the opposite usually happens. Every time a new tool removed some tedious task, designers ended up spending more time on the parts that actually needed a human, not less.

Auto layout meant less manually nudging things into alignment. Components meant less rebuilding the same thing over and over. This is just the next step. Less time spent on production work, more time spent on the stuff that actually needs a person’s judgment: understanding what users need, thinking through trade-offs, deciding what a product should even be doing in the first place.

What this actually means going forward

Nobody’s saying AI should design things instead of people. The more realistic version is AI designing alongside people. You describe a problem, the system asks a few good questions, it pulls up how similar things were solved before, it points out a possible issue before it becomes a real one, it puts together a first version, checks it’s accessible, and hands off something engineers can actually build from.

Not because it took the designer’s job.

Because it became the most useful teammate on the team.

Design systems have already gone through a few versions over the years: from simple style guides, to full component libraries, to what we have today. This is just the next step in that same story. Not a system that just tells you what exists, but one that actually helps you figure out what to build.

The value was never really about how many components were in the library. It was always about all the knowledge and decisions your team built up over time. We’ve spent years writing that down. Now we get to actually put it to work.
