Let me tell you about the day I found seventeen shades of grey.

Not on a moodboard. In production. Shipping to real users. Each one was supposed to be “our grey.” That was the moment I realised building a design system for a live product is nothing like the tidy case studies everyone loves to publish.

Almost every article assumes a clean slate: spin up a fresh repo, define your tokens, build your components in a quiet corner, done. But most of us do not get a clean slate. We inherit a product that is already live, already shipping features every week, already carrying years of well-intentioned decisions made by people who were just trying to hit a deadline. That is where the interesting, uncomfortable, rarely-discussed challenges actually live. Here are four of them.

## The audit will hurt your feelings

Everyone tells you to start with an inventory. Screenshot every button, every input, every card. What nobody tells you is how personal it gets. Those seventeen greys? Three date pickers built by three people who never talked to each other? Each one belongs to a real teammate who made a reasonable call under pressure. Walk into the room waving a list of everything that is broken and you have lost the team before you have written a single token. The audit is not a technical exercise. It is a diplomatic one. Frame it as look how much we can simplify together, not look at this mess you made.

You are changing the tyres while the car is doing 70

In a greenfield project, the design system and the product grow up together. On a live product, the ground never stops moving. The business still wants features shipped, bugs fixed, and roadmaps hit, all while you quietly try to swap the foundation underneath them. So adoption happens screen by screen, often right next to the very legacy components you are trying to kill. For months you live in a weird hybrid world where old and new sit side by side and nothing looks fully finished. That awkward in-between phase lasts far longer than any polished case study will admit. Make peace with it early.

## Buy-in is a bill that arrives every quarter

Getting the initial yes feels like the hard part. It is not. The hard part is defending the investment three months later, when the system has produced no flashy launch and no metric anyone can screenshot for a board deck. On a live product, the pressure to ship visible work never stops, and the design system is always the easiest thing to quietly deprioritise. Your survival skill becomes translation: turning we refactored our buttons into we ship features faster, with fewer bugs, and a more consistent brand. Tell that story once and it fades. Tell it every quarter and the system stays alive.

## Governance is where good systems quietly die

Launching version one is the fun part. Keeping it alive is not. Six months in, someone needs a component that does not exist yet, they are on a deadline, so they build a one-off. Fair enough. Do that twenty times and you are right back to seventeen greys, except now the chaos is hiding inside a system that proudly calls itself the source of truth. Real governance is deciding who can add to the system, how contributions get reviewed, and how you say no to exceptions without becoming the bottleneck everyone routes around. It is organisational design as much as interface design, and it is the part almost nobody plans for until it is already broken.

## So what actually makes it work

Building a design system for a live product has almost nothing to do with perfect components and almost everything to do with managing change inside a moving, messy, human organisation. The tokens and the Figma libraries are the easy part. The people, the politics, the slow migration, and the discipline to keep going long after the launch buzz fades: that is the real work. Go in expecting it, and you are already ahead of most teams.
